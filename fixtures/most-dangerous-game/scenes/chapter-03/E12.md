The sea was cold against his skin, the salt a raw abrasion in the wound on his cheek. Rainsford circled the island not as a man but as something older, something driven by a pulse that had forgotten the shape of mercy. The château rose from the cliff like a monument to appetite, every window a golden eye watching the dark. He found a drainpipe, climbed, and pulled himself onto a balcony. The bedroom beyond was empty. He slipped inside and became part of the shadows.

The curtains were velvet, thick enough to swallow a man. He pressed himself into their fold and waited. The château breathed around him—the crackle of settling timber, the distant clatter of dishes, the low murmur of a man speaking to servants. Rainsford counted his own heartbeats. They were slower now. Calmer. As if the water had washed something out of him and left only a clean, terrible stillness.

The door opened at ten. Zaroff entered with the unhurried ease of a man who has dined well, who has slept well every night of his life. He crossed to the window and threw back the casement, letting in the salt air. Below, the hounds stirred in their kennel, and he called down to them—a few words in a language Rainsford did not know, but whose meaning was unmistakable: *Rest. There is nothing left to hunt.*

Zaroff turned, and his hand found the switch. Light flooded the room.

Rainsford stepped from the curtains.

For an instant, the General’s face was a mask of pure astonishment—the eyes wide, the lips parted. Then the mask reassembled itself into something like admiration. He smiled.

“You have won the game,” he said. His voice was warm, almost tender. “You are a remarkable hunter, Rainsford.”

Rainsford did not smile. “I am still a beast at bay.”

Zaroff’s hand drifted toward the wall where a brace of pistols hung, then stopped. He seemed to consider something. Then he laughed—a soft, genuine sound.

“One of us is to furnish a repast for the hounds,” he said. “The other will sleep in this very excellent bed. On guard, Rainsford.”

They fought.

It was not a hunt. It was not a game. It was the meeting of two hungers in a space too small for both. Rainsford felt the wound in his cheek reopen, felt the General’s strength—surprising, terrible—and answered it with his own. The room became a blur of impact and breath, of silk tearing and wood groaning. Zaroff’s wounded shoulder betrayed him at the last. Rainsford saw the flinch, the fraction of a second, and took it.

When it was over, he stood above the General. The room was quiet. The hounds had stopped barking.

Rainsford turned down the coverlet and lay down. The sheets were cool and smooth.

He had never slept in a better bed.
