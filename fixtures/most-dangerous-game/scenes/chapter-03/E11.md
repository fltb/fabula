The hounds found the scent again.

Their baying erupted behind him, closer now—a dissonant chorus that echoed through the trees and set his heart hammering against his ribs. Rainsford ran. There was no thought left in him, only the raw animal imperative of flight. His lungs burned. His legs moved mechanically, each stride sending spikes of pain through his calves and thighs. The Malay mancatcher had bought him minutes, nothing more.

A gap of blue appeared through the lattice of branches ahead. Water. The sea.

He drove toward it, crashing through undergrowth that clawed at his torn clothes, his bare arms. The sound of the hounds swelled behind him, and with it, the crackling of men moving through brush—Zaroff's men, or Zaroff himself, pressing the chase. Rainsford did not look back. He could not afford the lost half-second.

The trees thinned. The ground fell away.

He emerged onto the cliff's edge so suddenly that he nearly pitched over it, his momentum checked only by a desperate, instinctive lurch backward. The stones crumbled beneath his feet, skittering into the void. Twenty feet below, the sea churned against black jagged teeth of rock, foam hissing white and then vanishing into the depths. The fall would break him. The rocks would finish what the fall began.

Across the cove, hunched against the pale sky like a beast at rest, stood the château. Its gray stone walls were slick with damp, its windows dark and empty. He had been there, inside those walls, only hours ago. It felt like another lifetime.

The baying grew louder.

Rainsford turned his head. Through the trees, no more than two hundred yards back, he saw the first of the hounds—a flash of russet fur, a mouth open wide, white teeth catching the light. Behind it, the shadows of men moved with purpose.

He had no knife. No weapon. No plan.

He looked down at the water again. The rocks gleamed wetly, waiting.

He leaped.

The air rushed past him. The cliff's edge receded above in a blur of stone and sky. For a moment he was suspended, weightless, his arms outstretched as if he might catch the wind and fly. Then the sea rose to meet him, and he plunged into its cold, salt-crusted body.

The impact was brutal. Something sharp caught his cheek—a rock, a hidden shelf—and he felt the skin part, felt the warmth of blood mingling instantly with the shock of frigid water. The cold seized his chest, forced the air from his lungs in a convulsive gasp. Salt burned in the wound.

He sank. The world became muffled and green, the light above fractured into shifting patterns. For a terrible second he did not know which way was up. Then his body remembered its purpose, and he kicked. His head broke the surface. He gasped, coughed, trod water.

Above him, the hounds reached the cliff's edge.
