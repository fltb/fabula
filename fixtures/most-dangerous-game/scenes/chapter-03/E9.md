The swamp swallowed the light before it swallowed him. Dusk came gray and sulfurous through the cypress knees, and the ground underfoot turned to something that breathed—a soft, sucking exhalation with every step. Rainsford felt it before he understood it: the bog pulling at his boots like a mouth tasting him.

His next step went through.

Not to solid earth but through—down into a cold grip that seized his calf and tugged. Quicksand. He threw himself backward, clawing at roots, at air, at anything, and the mud released him with a wet gasp. He lay on firmer ground, chest heaving, staring at the place where the earth had tried to hold him.

And then he smiled.

Because the earth could be made to hold something else.

He had dug foxholes in France—shallow, frantic scoops while shells whispered overhead and men died with their mouths open. But this was different. This was not shelter. This was a mouth of his own making.

He found a place where the ground was soft but firm enough to hold a shape. He began to dig. The blade of a rock, the cup of his hands, the gouging of his fingernails against roots. The hole grew slow and wet, filling with black water at the bottom until he bailed it with his cupped palms. Deeper. He went deeper until the walls rose above his shoulders and the sky became a ragged patch far overhead.

Then he cut saplings. Hard woods, springy and straight. He stripped them with his hunting knife—the knife still in his belt, thank God, thank God—and sharpened their ends against stone until they could take an eye.

He planted them in the pit floor. Points up. Waiting.

The covering took the longest. Weeds, moss, branches—layer on layer until the darkness of the hole was indistinguishable from the darkness of the ground. He crawled back from his work, brushing the evidence of his hands from the surface, and found his hiding place: a stump split by lightning, black and hollow, with room enough to crouch.

He settled into it. The pistol was cold against his ribs. The darkness settled around him like a second skin.

And then he heard them.

Not Zaroff.

The dogs first, their breathing ragged and eager. And behind them, the measured tread of boots—faster than before. Zaroff was hurrying. He was close now, too close, and Rainsford held his breath and watched the shadow of the general cross the clearing.

The ground gave way.

But it was not Zaroff who fell.

The hound went through the cover with a yelp that became a scream that became nothing. A wet sound. A settling. And then silence.

Rainsford stared at the pit. The stakes had found their mark.

From the darkness beyond, Zaroff's voice came low and warm, like a man applauding a clever move in chess.

"Congratulations, Rainsford. You have killed my best dog."

A pause. The rustle of fabric.

"I shall return with the pack."
