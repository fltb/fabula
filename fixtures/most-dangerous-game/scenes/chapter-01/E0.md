The deck rose and fell beneath them with the slow, rhythmic breathing of the sea. Above, no moon—only a scattering of stars like cold iron filings strewn across black velvet. The yacht cut through the Caribbean night with a surgeon’s precision, her bow whispering against water that was itself nearly invisible, a void textured only by the occasional phosphorescent curl of wake.

Rainsford leaned against the rail, his pipe cupped in his palm, the bowl warm against the damp air. The tobacco had a sweetness to it, Jamaican, he thought, or maybe Cuban—Whitney had brought it aboard in a pouch of oiled leather. He drew deep and let the smoke stream from his lips, watching it dissolve into the larger darkness.

“It’s a curious thing,” Whitney said from somewhere to his left. His voice had that quality it took on when he was about to say something Rainsford would find tedious—a softening at the edges, a turn toward the ruminative. “The way the crew speaks of it. Ship-Trap Island. They’ll not come within league of it at night, you know. The old hands cross themselves when the captain brings the charts out.”

Rainsford smiled around the stem of his pipe. “Sailors are a superstitious lot. The sea breeds it in them—too much time staring at horizons. They need monsters to explain the silence.”

“Perhaps.” Whitney paused. “But consider the jaguar, Rainsford. When we hunt it in the Amazon, do you think it feels nothing? The terror, the pain—are these not real to it?”

The question hung in the salt-stained air. Rainsford tapped the pipe against the rail, dislodging a small ember that hissed and died against the wet wood. “The jaguar feels what it needs to feel to survive. Fear is a mechanism, not a tragedy. The world is made up of two classes—the hunters and the huntees. Luckily, you and I are hunters.”

Whitney said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quieter. “Yes. Luckily.”

He knocked his pipe out against his palm, the small sound too sharp in the night. “I think I’ll turn in. Dawn comes early enough.”

Rainsford heard his footsteps retreat along the deck, the soft click of the cabin door, and then nothing but the water and the rigging and the distant creak of the ship’s bones.

He stood alone, drawing the last warmth from his pipe.

Then, from somewhere off the starboard bow—dark, unmistakable, tearing through the fabric of the night—came three gunshots.
