The dusk came on like a slow bleed through the canopy, the light draining green to grey to a deeper absence of color. Rainsford ran.

He ran not with the blind panic of a driven beast but with the cold calculation of a man who had spent years reading the signatures of flight—the bent blade of grass, the displaced leaf, the faint depression in soft earth where a hoof or a foot had fallen. He knew what a trail told, and he knew how to lie.

He doubled back through a dry creek bed, the gravel shifting under his boots, then cut hard east along a ridge of exposed rock where no print would hold. He looped in a wide, patient arc through a stand of mangrove-like trees, their aerial roots grasping at the dark soil like the fingers of drowned men. He waded upstream for a hundred yards in a shallow, cold stream, the water numbing his calves, the current erasing his passage. Then he climbed a fallen log and walked its length before dropping to the forest floor again, breaking his line.

His breath came in ragged, controlled bursts. The muscles of his thighs burned. The small food pack slapped against his hip. He did not stop.

He ran until the light failed entirely, until the jungle became a labyrinth of black shapes and deeper black absences, until the sounds of the day - the rustle of birds, the chatter of small things - gave way to the hum of insects and the distant, rhythmic pulse of the surf against the cliffs. Only then did he let himself slow.

He found the tree by touch more than sight. A great hardwood, its trunk thicker than a man's chest, its branches spreading in a broad, open crown. He climbed with the careful, economical movements of exhaustion, finding holds in the bark, pulling himself up through the darkness until he reached a fork where two massive limbs met the trunk. He settled into the crotch, his back against the bole, his legs drawn up, his hands resting on his knees. The knife was still at his belt.

He did not sleep. He waited. The jungle breathed around him, and he listened.

The sound came from the south - a soft, regular crunch of leaves, the measured tread of a man who was not hurrying. Rainsford's fingers closed around the knife hilt. He shifted his weight forward, peering down into the blackness.

A light bloomed below. The orange glow of a cigarette, cupped in a hand. Then the hand lowered, and the cigarette glowed again, and he saw the silhouette of General Zaroff outlined against the faint luminescence of the sky.

The general stopped beneath the tree. He studied the ground with the patience of a man reading a familiar book. Then he raised his head.

Rainsford watched the face tilt upward, inch by inch, the cigarette glowing and dimming. The eyes moved across the branches, pausing, searching. They reached Rainsford's branch. They stopped.

The general smiled. He took a long drag on the cigarette, held it, and then exhaled a perfect smoke ring that rose like a ghost through the leaves.

"Good night, Mr. Rainsford," he said softly, and walked on.

The horror settled over Rainsford not as a sudden shock but as a slow, drenching cold. He understood now. He was not being hunted. He was being *played*.
