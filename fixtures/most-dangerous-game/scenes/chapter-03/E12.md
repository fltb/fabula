The salt still clung to his skin, a fine grit that had worked itself into every crease and fold until he felt less a man than a piece of driftwood cast upon an alien shore. The château had risen before him like a fever dream, its towers black against the bruised sky, and he had found a way in—through a window left ajar, perhaps by some careless servant who could not imagine that the night's quarry might double back and breach the very walls of the hunter's keep. He had moved through corridors lined with trophies, his bare feet silent on the Persian rugs, and found the bedroom at last. The curtains were of heavy velvet, the color of old blood, and they received him as the sea receives the drowned.

He stood now behind them, breathing shallowly, his injured cheek pressed against the cool fabric. Through the gap he watched Zaroff enter, watched the general unbutton his coat with the unhurried precision of a man who has dined well and expects to sleep better. The man paused at the window, looked out at the courtyard, and whistled—a low, summoning note that brought the answering baying of the hounds. Zaroff smiled. He turned and switched on the light.

Rainsford stepped from the curtains.

The general's hand stopped mid-motion, frozen on its way to the collar of his shirt. For the space of two heartbeats, his face was a canvas of pure astonishment—the eyes wide, the mouth slightly parted. But the mask was quick to reassemble itself. He smiled, and it was almost genuine.

"Rainsford," he said, and the name seemed to please him. "You have won the game."

Rainsford shook his head. The motion cost him something; the world swam at the edges. "I am still a beast at bay."

Zaroff inclined his head, a gentleman acknowledging a point of etiquette. "One of us is to furnish a repast for the hounds. The other will sleep in this very excellent bed. On guard, Rainsford."

They fought.

It was not a clean thing, nor a noble thing. It was the thing that happens when the last boundaries have been crossed and there is nothing left but the animal truth of two bodies contending in a room that smelled of wine and leather and old death. Rainsford had no knife. He had no weapon but his hands and the fury that had carried him through the jungle and the sea and the dark. Zaroff was older, wounded in the shoulder, but he was strong and he was desperate, and desperation has its own kind of cunning.

But Rainsford had been the beast at bay longer.

When it was over, he stood above the general, breathing in great ragged gasps that tasted of copper and salt. He did not look at what his hands had done. He crossed to the great canopied bed, and he lay down upon it, and the sheets were cool and clean against his salt-crusted skin.

He had never slept in a better bed.
