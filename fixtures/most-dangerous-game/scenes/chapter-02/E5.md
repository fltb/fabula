The luncheon hall was a cathedral of white linen and silver, the light falling in broad sheets through tall windows that looked out onto nothing but green. Rainsford sat rigid at the table, the chair beneath him too soft, the air too still. He had not touched his plate.

Zaroff entered in country tweeds, the wool absurd against the tropical humidity, and settled himself across from Rainsford with the languid satisfaction of a man who has earned his meal. He spoke between bites of the night's hunt—a sailor who had hidden poorly, who had cried out when the dogs found him. The General yawned, covered his mouth with the back of his hand, and pronounced the affair thoroughly disappointing.

Rainsford set down his fork. The sound was louder than he intended.

"I want to leave this island."

Zaroff paused, a piece of meat suspended on his fork. He regarded Rainsford with mild curiosity, as one might examine a chess piece that had moved of its own accord.

"I'm afraid that is not possible."

The words were quiet, almost apologetic. But Rainsford saw the change in the General's eyes—the boredom sloughing away, something sharper waking beneath.

"There is, however, a choice." Zaroff placed his fork precisely beside his plate. "You will hunt with me tonight. You will be given a three-hour head start. Proper clothes. Food. A knife." He smiled, a thin crescent of politeness. "Or you will be given to Ivan."

Rainsford followed his gaze to the corner where the giant stood, motionless, his hands clasped before him. The man did not blink. He did not seem to breathe. He was a wall of flesh and silence, and Rainsford understood, with a clarity that settled cold in his stomach, what Ivan would do to him in the hours before dawn.

"Ivan," Zaroff said, almost gently, "is very patient."

Rainsford looked at the untouched food on his plate. He thought of the cellar. He thought of the jungle, dark and alive, and of the knife that might find a throat in the night.

"I'll hunt," he said.

Zaroff's face broke into something genuine, almost boyish in its delight. He clapped his hands once.

"At last. A foeman worthy of my steel."

The preparations were swift. Khaki trousers, a canvas shirt, a hunting knife in a leather sheath, a pack of dried meat and biscuit. Zaroff himself escorted him to the iron gate, past the chateau's manicured grounds and into the wall of green that rose like a second tide.

The gate swung open. The jungle exhaled its wet, patient breath.

"Three hours," Zaroff said.

Rainsford stepped through. Behind him, the lock clicked home.
