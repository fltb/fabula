He woke to a world of amber and shadow, the sun already fallen far into the western quarter of the sky. The hunger was a living thing now, a hollow ache that gnawed at his ribs, but the rest had done its work—his limbs felt less like lead, his mind less clouded. He rose from the sandy hollow where he had slept and looked out at the sea, flat and metallic under the declining light. No sign of the yacht. No sign of anything.

He turned inland and began to walk the shoreline, his bare feet flinching from the sharper stones. The jungle pressed close, rank and green, alive with the buzz and whir of unseen insects. He had gone perhaps a quarter mile when he found the first evidence that this island was not as empty as it had seemed.

The underbrush was crushed as if something large had been driven through it. The weeds at the margin of the trail were darkened with something that was not water. He knelt, touched the stain, and felt the faint tackiness of blood not yet fully dried. His hunter's eye took in the pattern: a heavy body, dragging itself, leaving a smear across the leaves.

And then, glinting in the late sun, the brass of an empty cartridge.

He picked it up, turned it over in his fingers. .22 caliber. A small round, the kind used for game—or for something that did not require a large hole. He pocketed it and pushed deeper into the brush.

The boot print was clear in a patch of damp earth: a heavy tread, a hunting boot, the heel distinct and the toe scuffed. He followed it. The trail led inland, climbing through the coastal scrub into the thicker timber of the island's interior. And as he walked, he began to notice the trees.

They had been stripped. Branches cut away, bark peeled in long spirals, the white wood gleaming like bone in the dusk. And on one, lashed with what looked like tendon or vine, a small skeleton hung—the bones of some animal, perhaps a dog or a goat, arranged in a shape that was almost a warning. He did not stop to study it. He knew a sign when he saw one.

The light was going fast now, the purple of twilight bleeding into the black of night. And then, through the trees, he saw it: a blaze of warm light, many windows, the outline of a vast roof against the sky. A château, impossibly grand, perched on a high bluff above the sea.

He climbed toward it, his breath ragged, his legs trembling with exhaustion. The gate was iron, topped with spikes, and as he gripped the bars and peered through, he felt a surge of something he had not allowed himself to feel since the water had closed over his head.

Hope.
