Rainsford’s fingers uncurled from the branch. He slid down the trunk, the bark scoring his palms, and landed in a crouch on the soft loam. Beneath the canopy the darkness pressed thick as a shroud, and the silence where Zaroff’s receding steps had been felt heavier than the man’s voice. Nausea coiled in his gut. *He is saving me*, Rainsford thought. *For tomorrow. For sport.* The saliva in his mouth turned bitter.

He forced himself to breathe. To think.

The Malay man-catcher. He had seen one used once in Sumatra—a deadfall triggered by a trip. Crude but patient. The jungle offered its own weapons if a man had the will to use them.

He moved through the dark with his hands out, feeling his way, until he found it: a huge dead tree, its roots half wrenched from the earth, slumped against a living ironwood like a drunk against a friend. The ironwood bent but held. He ran his palm along the dead trunk—dry, flaking, heavy with promise. His knife came out.

He worked without thought, his hands remembering what his mind could not yet afford to focus on. The scarf he had tied at his throat; he used it to lash a trigger bough across the game trail. He notched the dead tree’s base, wedged a forked branch under its weight, and ran a vine from the trigger to the fork. His fingers trembled but did not fumble.

An hour, perhaps more. The sky had not lightened when he finished. He retreated twenty feet and lay behind a fallen log, the moss cold against his cheek, the knife slick in his fist. *He will come back. He knows I am here. He will track me.*

And Zaroff did.

The general returned not with a crashing of brush, but with a hunter’s silence broken only by the deliberate fall of a boot on leaf litter. Rainsford pressed his face to the damp wood and watched through a crack in the log. Zaroff moved along the trail with his pistol held loosely, his head cocked, reading the ground as other men read books.

The trigger bough lay across his path.

Zaroff’s foot came down on it.

In the same instant his eyes widened—some bird’s silence, some shift in the air, some ancient reflex honed in a hundred bloody hunts. He threw himself backward.

The dead tree groaned and crashed down, its bulk shearing through the smaller branches of the ironwood, and it struck Zaroff—not full, not clean, but a glancing blow across the shoulder that spun him half around. He stumbled, caught himself, and straightened.

Then he laughed.

The sound was low and rich and utterly without anger.

“Rainsford,” he called into the darkness. “That was excellent. Truly. You are a hunter of the first order.”

Silence.

“I will dress this scratch and return at dawn. Rest well. Tomorrow will require all your ingenuity.”

He turned and walked away, one hand pressed to his shoulder, still chuckling.

Rainsford lay behind the log and did not move. His heart slammed against his ribs. The laughter echoed in the trees long after Zaroff had gone.
