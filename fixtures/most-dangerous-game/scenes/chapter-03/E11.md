The baying erupted behind him, closer now—the pack had found the trail again, their voices weaving through the trees like a tightening net. Rainsford drove his legs forward, each breath a blade in his chest, the food pack thudding against his spine. The jungle around him had thinned; he could see sky ahead, a blue gap between the trunks, and he aimed for it as a drowning man aims for the surface.

The ground fell away. He burst from the treeline onto a lip of rock and stopped, his heels skidding on the edge. Below him, twenty feet down, the sea churned against a chaos of black teeth—jagged shards of reef that broke the swell into white foam and sucking hollows. The water heaved and hissed, dark as slate, flecked with spume. Across the cove, rising from the island's shoulder like a tombstone, stood the château—its gray stone catching the last of the afternoon light, the windows dark and empty.

He had run in a circle. Or the island had bent him back. It didn't matter.

The hounds were close now—he could hear their panting, the crash of their bodies through the underbrush, the wet excitement in their throats. A man's voice called out, calm and clear, cutting through the din: a single word of command in Russian. The dogs surged.

Rainsford did not think. He leaped.

His body arced out over the rocks, arms spread, the air cold against his skin. For a moment he hung suspended, the sea below him a dark mirror, the château a distant smear of gray—and then he struck.

The water was not water but a wall. It drove the breath from him, the cold so immediate it felt like fire. A jagged edge of rock caught his cheek as he plunged, tearing through skin, and the taste of salt and blood flooded his mouth. He sank, limbs thrashing, the current tugging at him, the light above him a wavering silver coin.

He broke the surface gasping, sputtering, and pulled for the shelter of a ledge that overhung the water to his right. His arms felt like lead. The cold was eating into his bones. But he reached the shadow of the rock and clung there, his fingers numb against the barnacles, his breath coming in raw heaves.

Above, the hounds reached the cliff edge. He heard their frantic barking, the scrape of their claws on stone. Then Zaroff's voice, unhurried, almost bored: "Enough."

The barking subsided. Rainsford pressed himself deeper into the shadow, the water slapping at his chin, and looked up through the spray. Zaroff stood at the edge of the cliff, silhouetted against the pale sky, the wounded arm held stiff at his side. For a long moment he stared down at the churning water. Then he shrugged—a small, elegant gesture. He sat down on the grass, crossing his legs, and produced a flask from his pocket. He drank. He lit a cigarette, the match flaring in the gloom, and the smoke curled up around his head.

And then, faintly at first, Rainsford heard the general humming—a melody sweet and sorrowful, the aria of a woman waiting for her lover to return from the sea.

Rainsford crouched in the black water, his cheek bleeding, his body trembling, and did not dare to breathe.
