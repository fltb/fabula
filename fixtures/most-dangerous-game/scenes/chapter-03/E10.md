The first light was the color of a bruise, seeping through the canopy in sluggish, muted threads. Rainsford had not so much slept as collapsed against the bole of a kapok tree, his body a dead weight that his mind had simply abandoned for a time. The sound that tore him from this void was not a gradual thing. It came as a single, piercing note, then a chorus—the baying of hounds, not distant now, but close. Too close. The whole pack. Zaroff had grown tired of the game.

He was on his feet before his eyes were fully open, his legs screaming in protest, his lungs already burning. The sound was a physical force, a tide of noise that rolled through the jungle, shaking dew from the leaves and setting his heart to a frantic, useless rhythm. He had no plan. The food pack was a dead weight, but he could not bring himself to drop it. It was the only tangible remnant of his humanity, this small, absurd parcel.

Then, from the desperate, chaotic archive of a hunter's memory, an image surfaced. Uganda. A village elder. A thin, springy sapling. He saw it as if in a photograph: the bent wood, the lashing of vine, the blade aimed like an accusation down the narrow game trail.

He did not think. He acted.

His hands, raw and trembling, found a young tree of pale, supple wood. He forced it down, feeling its tensile life resist him. His hunting knife, the last of his tools, was in his hand. With a few savage twists, he bound the hilt to the bent sapling with a strand of wild grapevine, the blade pointing not at the trunk, but back along the trail, at the height of a man's chest. He stepped back, and the sapling held its breath, a coiled spring of patience and malice.

He ran.

The baying swelled, a hurricane of sound at his back. He did not look. He drove his legs, his breath a ragged sob in his throat. He stumbled, caught himself, and kept running. The sound was a physical weight on his shoulders, a hot breath on his neck.

And then it stopped.

The silence was more terrible than the noise. It was absolute, a vacuum into which all other sound was sucked—the birds, the insects, the very wind.

Rainsford found a tree, his fingers finding holds in the rough bark as if they had a will of their own. He climbed, his body moving by rote, until he was high enough to see.

The trail was a dark green tunnel below him. He saw the General first, standing in the middle of the path, his white shirt a startling beacon in the gloom. He was not moving. He was looking down.

Rainsford’s eyes followed the General’s gaze.

Ivan lay on the ground. The giant, the deaf-mute brute, was still, his bulk seeming to melt into the earth. And from his chest, at the exact height a man’s heart would be, the bone handle of Rainsford’s hunting knife jutted skyward, a grotesque, silent flower.

A wave of cold, pure horror washed through Rainsford, followed by a sharp, hollow absence at his belt. He was unarmed. He was a creature without fangs.
