Zaroff closed the door of the chateau behind him, and the latch clicked with a finality that vibrated through Rainsford’s bones. The last of dusk bled into the jungle’s edge; the air was thick and damp. Rainsford didn’t wait for the count, didn’t look back. He was already moving.

He did not run like a man fleeing—he ran like a fox, weaving, doubling, breaking his own trail at every clearing. He cut a tight loop around a thicket, then another, then dragged a fallen branch across his path to mask his prints. Every instinct born of years on the hunt screamed to make distance, but he forced himself to pause, to bend low, to lay a false scent along a stream before doubling back into the deep brush. He worked the jungle as if it were a chessboard, each move a gambit for confusion.

By the time night fell, his lungs burned and his legs trembled. He found a tree—a massive, gnarled thing with a trunk wider than a man’s reach and branches that splayed wide like a cathedral’s arms. He climbed, hand over hand, until he settled into a crotch where the bark cradled him. He pressed his back against the wood, knife in hand, breathing slow.

Sleep came in fragments, each broken by the snap of a twig or the scuttle of unseen things. He listened. The jungle hummed and clicked, alive with its own dark music.

Then he heard something else. The measured crunch of boots on leaf litter. He froze, peering down through the gloom.

Zaroff emerged like a shadow coalesced into form. He held a small automatic pistol, and its barrel caught a sliver of starlight. He studied the ground where Rainsford’s trail dissolved into confusion. For a long moment, he was still.

Then he looked up.

Inch by inch, his gaze traveled the trunk, the lower branches, the middle, rising toward where Rainsford crouched, heart hammering. The general’s eyes were calm, appraising. They climbed, and climbed, and stopped just short of Rainsford’s branch.

Zaroff smiled. He raised a cigarette to his lips, drew, and exhaled a perfect smoke ring that rose into the dark. Then he turned and walked away, his footsteps fading into silence.

Rainsford sat motionless, the knife slick in his palm. He understood now, with a clarity that hollowed him out: Zaroff had seen him from the start. The trail, the tree, the hunted pause—none of it had deceived. The general was not tracking. He was savoring.

The game was not a chase. It was a slow, deliberate mercy—and the hunter had every intention of extending it.
