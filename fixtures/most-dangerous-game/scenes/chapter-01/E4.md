The dining hall was a cathedral of death. Rainsford stood at the threshold, his borrowed clothes still stiff from the salt spray that had dried upon them hours ago, and watched the candlelight play across the walls. The flames, set in iron sconces shaped like grasping hands, cast long shadows that seemed to breathe. Above them, row upon row of glass eyes stared into the darkness—the heads of ibex and lion, of bear and buffalo, of creatures he had pursued across four continents. They lined the walls in tiers, their antlers and horns interlocking like the branches of a petrified forest. The air smelled of beeswax and blood, though the blood was old, cured into the wood.

Zaroff gestured to a chair at the head of a table long enough to seat thirty. "Please. You must be famished."

Rainsford sat. The chair was carved from oak so dark it seemed to drink the light. Ivan appeared with a silver tureen, his massive hands steady as he ladled borsch into bowls. The soup was deep crimson, almost black in the dim light, and it tasted of beets and dill and something Rainsford could not name—some spice that lingered on the tongue like a question.

"You are wondering about the island," Zaroff said, dipping his spoon. "The ships. The men." He smiled, and the candlelight caught the gold of his teeth. "I will tell you everything. But first—eat. A hunter cannot think on an empty stomach."

They ate. Filet mignon, pink at the center, butter pooling on the plate. The champagne arrived in a bucket of chiseled ice, and the port came later, dark as the wood of the table. And as they ate, Zaroff talked.

He spoke of his youth in the Urals, of riding with the Cossacks across the steppe, of the first wolf he had killed with his own hands—a gray shadow that had turned at bay and shown him something in its eyes that he had spent his life trying to find again. He spoke of his service in the Tsar's army, of the hunts he had organized for the nobility, of the years after the revolution when he had wandered the world with a rifle and a thirst that nothing could quench. He had shot tiger in Bengal and elephant in the Congo. He had stalked the snow leopard through the Hindu Kush and the grizzly through the Rockies. He had killed every animal that walked or crawled or flew.

"And then," Zaroff said, setting down his glass, "I grew bored."

The word hung in the air like smoke. Rainsford felt something cold move through him.

"Boredom is the disease of the intelligent," Zaroff continued. "I had mastered every beast. I knew their tracks, their habits, their fears. They were predictable. They had no cunning—not real cunning. They acted on instinct, and instinct can be anticipated. I needed something new. Something that could reason. Something that could hope."

He leaned forward. His eyes were very clear, very blue.

"So I invented a new animal."

Rainsford's throat tightened. "What are you saying?"

"You know what I am saying." Zaroff's voice was soft, almost tender. "I hunt men."

The word came out before Rainsford could stop it. "Murder."

"Murder?" Zaroff laughed, a low sound like the rumble of distant thunder. "My dear fellow, you are a hunter. You have killed countless animals. Were they murder? No. They were sport. Life is for the strong. The weak exist to be culled—that is the law of nature, the law I have chosen to follow. I do not kill for food, or for survival. I kill for the hunt itself. And there is no prey so challenging as man."

Rainsford pushed back from the table. The chair scraped against the stone floor. "I will not hunt with you."

Zaroff studied him for a long moment. Then he smiled again, but his eyes did not smile.

"We shall see. Ivan will show you to your room. Sleep. You will need your strength."

Ivan stepped forward, his hand resting on the revolver at his sash. Rainsford had no choice but to follow. They climbed a winding staircase, passed doors of ironbound oak, and stopped at the end of a narrow hall. Ivan unlocked a door and gestured inside. The room was comfortable—a bed, a fireplace, a window that looked out on the sea.

Rainsford turned to close the door. The lock clicked. He tried the handle, and found it would not turn.

He was trapped.
