The general had gone, melting back into the trees with a hand clamped to his shoulder, and Rainsford did not wait to see him go further. He ran. The Malay moccasins were still on his feet; the food pack still hung at his hip. These facts seemed to him, in that moment, almost miraculous. He ran until his lungs burned and the blood sang in his ears, and when his legs would carry him no farther he walked, and when walking became a kind of slow-motion nightmare he stumbled forward with one hand braced against the trunks of ancient mangroves.

Dusk came softly, then withdrew into night.

The darkness was absolute. No stars penetrated the canopy. He moved by touch and by the memory of the land that had already begun to betray him, his fingers reading bark and vine like a blind man reading a page. The ground changed underfoot without warning: the hard-packed earth of the hunting trail softened, yielding like the flesh of something recently dead. He slowed, cautious, and the next step sank ankle-deep into a black mire that sucked at his foot with a low, glutinous sigh.

He froze. The moccasin was held fast, and beneath it there was no bottom—only a cold, yielding nothing that pulled with the patience of gravity. Quicksand.

He wrenched his leg free with a gasp and fell backward, his heart hammering against his ribs. He lay there for a long moment, panting, feeling the damp earth against his back, and then the idea came to him. It came not as a flash of inspiration but as a slow, cold certainty, the way a hunter learns to read the land and knows what it will yield.

The soft earth would yield a pit.

He worked in darkness, his hands finding the shapes of things—the fibrous roots, the fallen branches, the young saplings with their bark still green and their tips sharp enough to draw blood. He snapped them, stripped them, and set them in the hole he had clawed from the mire, their points angled upward like the teeth of a great and patient thing. He wove a cover of weeds and branches and sprinkled it with loose soil until the hole was invisible, till the ground looked as it had before he came—just another patch of the swamp, treacherous and still.

Then he found the charred stump of a lightning-blasted tree, and he crouched behind it, and he waited.

The swamp breathed around him. Frogs called and fell silent. Somewhere an insect sang its single note again and again, a small and mindless clock.

Then he heard the footsteps.

They came with unusual swiftness, deliberate and sure, cutting through the undergrowth with a confidence that Rainsford did not share. A single set of feet, moving fast. Too fast for a man who had been wounded.

The cover gave way with a crack and a terrible thrashing—but the cry that rose from the pit was not human. It was a dog’s howl, cut short in a way that Rainsford would remember for the rest of his life.

Silence.

And then the general’s voice, drifting through the dark with a note of dark amusement.

“You have scored again, Mr. Rainsford.”

A pause.

“I shall return with the whole pack.”
