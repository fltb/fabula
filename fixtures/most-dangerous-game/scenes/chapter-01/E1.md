The sound cracked the night open—three shots, sharp and distinct, ripping through the damp Caribbean air. Rainsford was at the rail before his mind had finished registering them, his pipe clenched between his teeth, his eyes straining into the absolute dark where the reports had come from. Nothing. Only blackness and the faint phosphorescent kiss of waves against the hull.

Something whipped past his face. A rope end, loose from the rigging, swung on the yacht's gentle roll. His pipe was gone—knocked from his mouth, spinning end over end into the void. He lunged after it, a hunter's reflex, the instinct to recover what was his. The rail caught him at the hip and kept going.

There was a moment of perfect suspension, his body cantilevered over nothing, and then the sea took him.

The water was blood-warm. It closed over his head with hardly a shock, and when he broke the surface, gasping, the yacht was already sliding away into the darkness, its lights shrinking to scattered stars. He treaded water, watching it go. No shout went up. No one had seen.

He did not curse. He did not rage. Rainsford was not a man who wasted energy on the irretrievable. He turned in the water, orienting himself by the memory of the shots, and began to swim.

The clothes were weights. He stripped them off one-handed, treading water, letting them sink into the abyss below. Naked now, skin to sea, he struck out toward the black mass that he knew must be land. Ship-Trap Island. The name had meant little to him over dinner. Now it was everything.

A sound came across the water—an animal cry, high and terrified, cut short by the flat bark of a pistol. Rainsford's stroke quickened. The hunter in him understood that sound in his bones. A kill. Something had died in that instant, and whatever had killed it was still there, on that island, holding a weapon.

The swim became a thing without time. His arms burned. His legs grew leaden. The sea was warm but his breath came in ragged gasps, and the shore refused to come closer. He swam. He had no other choice.

His hand scraped rock. Coral, sharp as broken glass, raked his palm. He crawled forward, dragging himself out of the sucking surge, onto stone that cut his knees and tore his skin. The island rose above him, cliffs and jungle black against a blacker sky.

He made it clear of the water's reach. And there, on the jagged shore, with the salt drying on his back and the blood welling from a dozen small wounds, Sanger Rainsford let the darkness take him.
